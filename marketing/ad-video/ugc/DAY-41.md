# "Day 41": two-minute phone-shot film

A two-minute vertical film shot like someone's own phone footage: a working mother and her son living in their
car, the night she finally asks for help, and the stranger who answers.

**Made (Nov 2026):** the illustrated, voiced version is built in this repo (`../versions/day41.html`,
`../out/day41-9x16.mp4`, `-16x9`, `-1x1`), labelled "Dramatization · illustrated characters · synthetic voices".
It was made here because no outside generator was to be used and photoreal people cannot be generated in this
environment. The two routes below are still open if a live-action or photoreal version is wanted later:

- **Route A, real people (recommended).** A real creator and a real family with an open fundraiser on
  CouponDonation, filmed with consent. This is what actually goes viral, and it needs no disclaimer beyond "#ad".
- **Route B, AI-generated actors.** The same beats with generated people, always labelled on screen as a
  dramatization. Never published as if the people were real.

## What the research says

- **The viral ones are real.** GoFundMe's biggest moments come from creators filming real strangers: the Coney
  Island "Byron" clip, a student raising about $400K for a man with cancer. Structure every time: a short street
  encounter, one human detail, the creator does one small kind thing on camera, the fundraiser is named at the
  end, follow-up videos drive the giving.
- **They blow up when the facts are shaky.** In the $400K case the creator was listed as beneficiary, donors
  demanded refunds and GoFundMe froze the money. Another creator was accused of mishandling $20K.
  Trust is the whole product.
- **GoFundMe's own brand film used AI, on real stories.** "Year In Help" generated its visuals with AI, but every
  scene was a real fundraiser and viewers could click through to it.
- **Rules.** The FTC's 2024 rule bans testimonials from people who don't exist or didn't have the experience,
  AI-generated ones included. YouTube requires disclosure of realistic synthetic content a viewer could mistake
  for a real person or event.

Our edge over the creator-run GoFundMe model: on CouponDonation the family has its own fundraiser, the gift
becomes gift cards for the stores they chose, and the creator never touches the money. Say that.

## The line we hold

AI-generated people are never presented as real people in need, and no invented person "testifies" to using
CouponDonation. That is fake-beneficiary fundraising: it breaks the FTC rule, platform policy and our own claims
rules (`AGENTS.md`), and one screenshot of it would cost more donors than the ad could ever bring. Route B is
therefore labelled start to finish, and its narrator states only how the site really works.

About 250 spoken words. In Route B the corner label reads **"Dramatization · AI-generated actors, not real people"**

**Why it should travel:** a six-word hook with a secret in it; a parent who works and still can't cover rent
(no one can dismiss her); the child protecting the parent, which is the beat people share; the viewer cast as the
unnamed stranger; an ending that thanks them before they have given.

**Claims check:** from $10, 100% reaches families, no platform fee, gifts become store gift cards, and the gift
arrives days later (the team sizes coupons by hand), all per `AGENTS.md`. Nothing about housing, tax deductions,
charities or verified families. Before filming, match the "new gift" wording on her phone to the real email.

**Cutdowns:** 0:30 (hook, "not that hungry", submit, groceries, end card) and 0:15 (hook, groceries, end card).

## Route A: real creator, real family

1. **Find the family.** Ask organizers of open Food & groceries fundraisers, through the team thread
   (Admin > Fundraisers > Contact & emails), whether they would tell their story on camera. Written release, the
   right to withdraw, and they approve the final cut. Pay them for their time separately from any gifts.
2. **Hire the creator.** A UGC creator (Billo, Insense, Collabstr; roughly $150–400 a video) films with them for a
   day, or the family films themselves and the creator narrates.
3. **Keep the beats, drop the lines.** The table above becomes prompts: "What does a morning look like?", "What did
   you put back this week?", "What was it like to press submit?" Their own words replace Renee's.
4. **The creator gives on camera** with their own card (screen recording), and says so honestly. Caption carries
   "#ad" or the paid-partnership label.
5. **End on the real fundraiser link** (`/f/…`), so every viewer can check the story and give to that family.
6. **Dignity rules:** no filming anyone without consent, no faces of children without a parent's release, no
   staging hardship, no surprise "reveals" that put someone on the spot.

## Route B: AI-generated actors (labelled)

Fifteen eight-second clips (Veo 3 with native audio, 9:16) cut to the table above, or generated stills animated
shot by shot. Keep one character sheet and reuse it in every prompt so faces stay consistent:

- **Renee:** woman, mid-thirties, tired eyes, hair in a loose bun, grey hoodie under a work polo, no makeup.
- **Her son:** boy of eight, short curly hair, navy hoodie, red-and-grey backpack.
- **Look:** "vertical phone video, handheld, slight shake, natural light, no colour grade, no captions, no logos".

Example prompt (0:00): *"Vertical phone selfie video from the driver's seat of an older sedan at dawn, breath
visible, windows fogged. [Renee] whispers to camera: 'It's day forty-one. He still thinks we're camping.' Handheld,
natural light, no captions."*

The on-screen label, the screen recording of the real apply flow, captions and the end card are added in the
edit (`../lib/stage.js` can burn them in), not generated. When uploading to YouTube, tick "altered or synthetic
content". Do not post Route B anywhere without the label, and do not cut the label out of clips.
